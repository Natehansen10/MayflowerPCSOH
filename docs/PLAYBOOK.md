# The Playbook

Why this was built the way it was, and what to do with it for the next twelve
months. The goal is one signed custom-build contract. Everything here serves
that.

---

## The problem with the obvious approach

The instinct at a home show is to maximize entries: a giveaway, a short form, a
QR code at the door, a big list at the end of the week.

That optimizes for the wrong person. Of five to ten thousand visitors, the number
of households that could actually commission a $5M+ custom home in Park City is
probably **twenty to fifty**. A raffle entry form is a volume instrument pointed
at a precision problem, and worse, it is mildly repellent to exactly the buyer
that matters. People who write seven-figure checks do not enter drawings.

So the design goal was inverted: **build something that most visitors will
politely ignore and that the right twenty will find genuinely useful.** Every
choice follows from that.

---

## Why radical transparency, and why it can't be copied

At this price point the buyer is not choosing a house. They are choosing a person
to hand eighteen months and seven figures to. Their real fears are specific and
well-founded: the budget will move, the schedule will slip, and they will find
out about both after the fact.

Every luxury builder answers those fears with photography. Photography is
undifferentiated — every competitor at the showcase has a good photographer, and
the buyer has already seen a thousand beautiful kitchens.

Almost nobody shows the money. The Build File does: what each decision cost, what
a low bid quietly removes, and — most importantly — **what went wrong on this
build and how it was handled**. The three change orders Mayflower ate. The rock
at six feet. The stair drawn three times.

That last category is the moat. A competitor can hire a better photographer next
week. A competitor cannot casually copy "here are the mistakes we made and what
they cost us," because saying it requires actually having handled them that way.
It is also the single most credible thing a two-person GC can say, and it costs
nothing to produce.

**The mechanical room is the centerpiece on purpose.** It is the room every
builder walks guests past. Stopping there, with a price on the wall, is a
statement about what kind of company this is that no headline could make.

## Why a configurator instead of a form

The estimator does four jobs at once, which is why it earned the week:

1. **It qualifies without interrogating.** Someone who spends four minutes
   configuring a 9,000 sf Legacy-finish home on a steep Deer Valley lot has told
   you their budget, their taste, their timeline and their seriousness — without
   ever being asked "what's your budget?", a question that makes this buyer
   close the tab.
2. **It trades something real for the email.** A giveaway trades a lottery ticket.
   This trades a cost range, a schedule, and a draw curve — information they
   currently cannot get without paying an architect first. That is a fair trade
   at this price point, and it makes the follow-up welcome rather than intrusive.
3. **It arms the first conversation.** Curtis calls already knowing what they
   want to build. There is no discovery call.
4. **It filters in both directions.** Someone at $1.8M finds out early. So does
   Mayflower. Neither wastes a season.

The results are shown **before** the email is asked for. Gating them would have
converted slightly better and cost more than it earned — a buyer at this level
reacts badly to being held up for contact information.

The anonymous configuration signal (written to the **Signals** tab) means you
also learn what visitors are configuring even when they never hand over a name.
After the week, that tab tells you what the Park City market walked in wanting.

## Lead tiers, and what each one is worth

| | Who | What it means | The action |
|---|---|---|---|
| **A** | 70+ points, building for themselves | A live prospect. Probably five to fifteen of these all week. | **Curtis calls within 24 hours.** Never an email. |
| **B** | 45–69 | Real, not urgent yet. | Personal email in 48 hours, then the cadence. |
| **C** | under 45 | Early or dreaming. | Long, cheap nurture. Some of these are A leads two years from now. |
| **R** | Agents | A channel, not noise. | Referral-partner track. |
| **T** | Builders, designers, suppliers | Competitors and trades. | Be gracious. Spend nothing. |

Scoring weights implied budget, lot ownership, timeline, whether they asked for a
walk, how many build details they saved, and whether they wrote a real note.
It is deliberately crude. It only has to be right enough to sort a phone list.

**The single highest-signal field on the site is the "walk a lot with us"
checkbox.** Someone who checks it is asking for an hour with a builder. In this
business that is nearly the whole funnel in one click. Treat it as an emergency.

---

## Why "walk a lot with us" is the offer instead of "get a free quote"

A quote requires drawings they don't have yet, so it's not really an offer — it's
a request for work on their part.

Walking a lot is different. It's an hour, it's free, it's genuinely useful even
if they never hire Mayflower, it happens on Mayflower's home ground where the
expertise is obvious and unfakeable, and it is impossible to do without building
a relationship. It also reaches people **before** the architect does, which is
the moment a builder has the most influence over the eventual outcome and the
least competition.

The offer to tell someone a lot is a mistake is not a flourish. It is the proof
that the advice is worth taking.

## The twelve months after the show

This is where the contract is actually won. A build this size runs a 6–18 month
sales cycle, so anything that only works during showcase week is theater.

The system schedules a cadence per tier at capture, and every morning at 6am
prepares that day's touches as **Gmail drafts** with a digest listing what's due.

**Drafts, not automated sends** — that's a deliberate and important choice. This
buyer can tell the difference between a person and a sequence, and getting caught
running a drip campaign at someone about to spend $6M is worse than never
emailing them. The system removes the *remembering*, not the *writing*. Curtis
reads each one, changes a line, sends it. Two minutes a day.

The Tier A cadence, and why each touch exists:

| Day | Touch | The job it does |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Personal follow-up | Be the first builder to call. Most never do. |
| 4 | A real allowance schedule | Hands them a weapon to use against every other bid. Builds trust by arming them against you. |
| 14 | How draws and lien releases work | Answers the fear they haven't voiced: "will I lose control of the money?" |
| 45 | What went wrong on the showcase house | Pre-frames the inevitable problem, so that when a competitor's job blows up they think of you. |
| 90 | Lots | Re-opens the highest-value conversation. |
| 150 | The schedule math | The only honest urgency lever in this business: to move in by a given season, design has to start by a specific month. Not a deadline — arithmetic. |
| 240 | Three references, in order | Not a curated list. The last three owners, and "ask them what went wrong." |
| 330 | A year on | Re-engage or let go cleanly. |

Nothing in that sequence is "just checking in." Every touch carries something
worth opening. That's what makes an eleven-month cadence tolerable instead of
irritating.

## What to measure

Not visitor counts. Not scans. Those feel like progress and aren't.

**Weekly, during and after the show:**
- Tier A leads captured
- Walk requests → **walks actually scheduled** (the only number that matters)
- Walks → design agreements

**One number decides whether this worked:** did a walk turn into a signed build
agreement within twelve months? Everything else on the dashboard is instrumentation.

A realistic shape for the week: 5,000–10,000 visitors across the whole tour, a
few hundred through this house who scan something, perhaps 80–200 who finish the
configurator, 10–25 Tier A/B, 5–15 walk requests, 3–8 walks that actually happen,
one or two that go to design. **One contract is the win.**

---

## What was deliberately left out

- **A giveaway.** Wrong buyer, and it degrades the brand at exactly the moment
  the brand is being judged.
- **Instant chat / an AI concierge in the house.** People on a self-guided tour
  with their family don't want to type at a robot, and a chatbot's failure mode
  in front of a $6M prospect is severe.
- **Gated results.** Costs more than it earns at this price point.
- **A CRM.** Two people, a spreadsheet, and a phone list beat a CRM nobody logs
  into. Everything exports the day that stops being true.
- **Anything with a monthly bill.** Not because of the money — because a
  subscription nobody renews is a lead list that dies quietly in eighteen months.
